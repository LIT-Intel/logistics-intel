# How Freight Brokers Find Shippers in 2026 (7 Proven Methods)

Finding shippers is the hardest — and most important — part of running a freight brokerage. Carriers are easy to source; **shippers with real, recurring freight are what actually build a book.** The brokers who win aren't necessarily the best salespeople; they're the ones with the best *list* of who to call and *why* to call them right now.

This guide breaks down the seven ways freight brokers find shippers in 2026, what each is actually good for, and where the modern approach — using customs and bill-of-lading data to see who's shipping *before* you pick up the phone — changes the math.

## The short answer

Freight brokers find shippers through a mix of **outbound prospecting** (cold calls, email, LinkedIn), **inbound and relationship channels** (referrals, associations, existing carriers), and **data-driven prospecting** (lead lists and, increasingly, customs/bill-of-lading shipment data that reveals which companies are actively importing and moving freight). The fastest-growing method is the last one, because it lets you target companies you already know have freight — instead of dialing blind.

## 1. Cold calling

Still the backbone of freight sales, and still effective when the list is good. The problem was never the phone — it's *who you're calling.* Dialing a random manufacturer with no idea whether they ship 5 or 5,000 loads a year is how reps burn out. Cold calling works best when it's the *last* step after you've already identified an active shipper and the right contact.

- **Good for:** direct conversations, fast feedback, relationship building.
- **Weak spot:** wasted dials on companies with little or no relevant freight.

## 2. Load boards

DAT, Truckstop, and others show freight that's *already posted* — which means it's already being handled by someone else. Load boards are useful for spot capacity and understanding lane rates, but they're a weak *prospecting* channel: you're seeing the freight after the shipper already chose a provider.

- **Good for:** spot lanes, rate benchmarking, carrier sourcing.
- **Weak spot:** you're late to the shipper relationship by definition.

## 3. Referrals and existing relationships

Your current shippers, carriers, and industry contacts are the highest-conversion source of new shippers — a warm introduction beats any cold list. The limitation is volume: referrals don't scale on their own, and you can't build a pipeline plan around hoping for them.

- **Good for:** high close rates, trust, faster onboarding.
- **Weak spot:** unpredictable, low volume.

## 4. Trade associations and events

Industry associations (TIA, regional shipper groups) and trade shows put you in the room with logistics decision-makers. Great for credibility and a few strong leads per event, but expensive and slow relative to the pipeline most brokerages need.

- **Good for:** credibility, deep conversations, niche verticals.
- **Weak spot:** cost and cadence — you can't prospect this way every week.

## 5. LinkedIn and social selling

LinkedIn is where logistics and supply-chain decision-makers actually are. Following target accounts, engaging with their posts, and reaching out with a relevant angle works — *if* you already know which companies to target and have a reason to reach out. On its own, LinkedIn is a channel, not a list.

- **Good for:** warming contacts, reaching decision-makers directly.
- **Weak spot:** needs a target list and a relevant hook to be efficient.

## 6. Purchased lead lists

Generic B2B databases (ZoomInfo, Apollo, Seamless) give you contacts and firmographics, but they're built for *all* B2B sales — they don't tell you who's actually moving freight, on which lanes, or how often. You get names, not freight signal, so qualification still happens on the phone.

- **Good for:** contact coverage at scale.
- **Weak spot:** no freight context — you still don't know who actually ships.

## 7. Customs and bill-of-lading data (the modern method)

Every ocean import into the U.S. generates a **bill of lading** — a public record of who imported what, from which supplier, through which port, and when. Aggregated, this data lets a broker see **which companies are actively shipping, on which lanes, and at what volume — before making a single call.** Instead of dialing blind, you build a list of proven, active shippers and walk into every conversation already knowing their freight.

This is the shift behind tools like **Logistics Intel**: search bill-of-lading and customs records by industry, geography, lane, commodity, and volume to surface active importers; see each company's lanes, suppliers, products, and shipment cadence; enrich the verified decision-maker's contact; and move the account straight into a freight-native CRM to work it. It turns "who should I call?" into "here are 40 companies importing on your lane this quarter, and the person to reach."

- **Good for:** targeting *proven* active shippers, lane-specific prospecting, walking in informed.
- **Weak spot:** ocean/customs data is strongest for importers; pair it with the channels above for domestic-only shippers.

## Which method should you use?

The best-run brokerages don't pick one — they **stack** them: use customs/bill-of-lading data to build a list of active shippers on their target lanes, enrich the right contact, then reach out by phone, email, and LinkedIn with a specific, freight-aware hook. Data tells you *who and why*; the human channels close. The old way was to dial first and qualify later; the modern way is to qualify with data first, then dial with a reason.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do freight brokers find shippers?**
Freight brokers find shippers through cold calling, load boards, referrals, trade associations, LinkedIn, purchased lead lists, and — increasingly — customs and bill-of-lading data that reveals which companies are actively importing and moving freight. The most efficient approach combines data-driven targeting (to find proven active shippers) with direct outreach (to win them).

**What is the fastest way to find active shippers?**
The fastest way is to start from shipment data rather than cold lists. Customs and bill-of-lading records show which companies are importing right now, on which lanes, and at what volume — so you target proven shippers instead of guessing. Tools like Logistics Intel let you search that data, enrich the right contact, and start outreach the same day.

**Can freight brokers see who is importing?**
Yes. U.S. ocean imports generate bill-of-lading records that identify the importer, supplier, product, port, and date. Platforms that aggregate this data let brokers search by company, commodity, lane, or volume to find active importers to prospect.

**Do load boards help find shippers?**
Load boards are useful for spot capacity and rate benchmarking, but they're a weak prospecting channel — the freight is already posted, meaning the shipper has already chosen a provider. To find shippers *before* they commit, brokers use customs/bill-of-lading data and direct outreach.

**What data do freight brokers use to prospect?**
The most valuable prospecting data for freight brokers is customs and bill-of-lading shipment data (who's shipping, on which lanes, how often), enriched with verified decision-maker contacts. This is more actionable than generic B2B contact databases because it shows real freight activity, not just company names.
